Abstract
Accidental falls have emerged as a major public health concern, especially among individuals aged 65 and older, due to 
their high incidence and severe consequences. Without timely intervention, such falls can result in fractures, traumatic 
brain injuries, and long-term complications. As a result, considerable research has focused on developing automated fall 
detection systems that integrate intelligent algorithms with sensor-based data acquisition to enable rapid response and 
medical assistance. This study follows the PRISMA framework to conduct a systematic literature review. A comprehensive 
search was performed across major databases including PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore using fall 
detection–related keywords. From an initial pool of 596 articles, duplicates were removed and strict inclusion/exclusion 
criteria were applied, resulting in 182 relevant articles for in-depth analysis. This review examines a wide range of Arti -
ficial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) approaches applied to fall detection using diverse sensor modalities, 
including wearable, vision-based, ambient, and multimodal systems. Additionally, it summarizes the publicly available 
datasets and sensor configurations used in existing studies. This broad perspective is essential to compare trade-offs across 
sensing modalities and support effective system development for real-world deployment. This review provides a com -
parative overview of AI/ML-based fall detection approaches, highlighting differences in accuracy, sensitivity, and dataset 
usage as reported in existing literature. In the end, many open research challenges in using AI and ML to detect falls are 
outlined, along with potential future perspectives.
Keywords Wearable sensor · Vision · Ambient · Sensor fusion · Machine learning · Deep learning

Introduction
The world is beginning to observe a prolonged change 
in demographic age distribution, owing to the increasing 
average lifespan and decreasing birth rate trends. People 
live longer lives now than before. The percentage of older 
people in the overall population is growing tremendously. 
In 2020, 727 million people worldwide were reported to be 
65 or older [ 1]. The global elderly population is projected 
to almost double over the next three decades, reaching over 
1.5 billion by 2050 [ 1]. Nearly every region of the world 
will experience an increase in the ageing population by 
2050. The percentage of people aged 65 and up is expected 
to rise from 9.3% to 16.0% within thirty years [ 2]. Ageing 
is often associated with deteriorating functionalities, such 
as physiological, visual, and cognitive impairments, which 
increase the likelihood of falling. Around 28-35 per cent 
of elderly individuals aged 65 or above every year are esti -
mated to fall [3]. As age and frailty increase, the frequency 
of falls significantly increases. An estimated 646,000 fatal 
falls occur annually, making it the second leading cause of 
mortality by accidental injuries after road accidents [ 4]. 
Nearly 37.3 million falls each year require to receive med -
ical treatment [ 5].
More than 17 million disability-adjusted life years 
(DALYs) have been lost as a result of these falls [ 4]. Fall-
related deaths account for almost 80% of deaths in low and 
middle-income countries, with 60% of these deaths hap -
pening in Southeast and Western Pacific Asia [ 6]. Senior 
citizens over 70 years who fall each year and suffer serious 
or minor medical injuries are estimated to be between 32 
to 42 per cent [ 7]. Fall is one of the unexpected occur -
rences in regular activities, impacting individual health. 
Fall events demonstrate loss of voluntary body control and 
drastic shifts in body acceleration. Additionally, fall occur -
rences create monetary and mental pressure on the caregiv-
ers and decrease life quality. Early detection of falls will 
help to reduce severe effects and support healthcare [ 8–12] 
sector. Fall recognition and prevention are two strategies 
for responding earlier to elderly fall events. Researchers 
studied these strategies, particularly various fall detec -
tion methods explored and discovered over the past two 
decades. Different Artificial Intelligence and Machine 
Learning [ 13–23] algorithms are examined alongside the 
utilisation of multiple kinds of sensors for fall detection 
(see Fig. 1).
A significant number of literature reviews are per -
formed on fall detection and prediction systems. Most 
review works cover specified categories and technologies 
implemented for fall detection and prevention. Pannurat 
et al. [24] reviewed existing elderly fall detection schemes 
based on wearable and ambient sensors, prioritising wear -
able sensor-based systems. The authors also analysed 
commercially available wearable fall-detecting devices 
and studied the most useful statistical features imple -
mented. A systematic literature review has been carried 
out [25] to investigate wearable elderly fall detection sys -
tems. These were differentiated into three groups: fall risk 
assessment, fall type identification, and fall events detec -
tion. The authors studied previously selected research 
questions among 29 selected peer-reviewed journals and 
analysed the cutting-edge practice in the existing systems. 
Limitations and research gaps of the studied articles are 
discussed, along with scopes and directions for future 
research. Chaudhuri et al. [ 26] presented a systematic lit -
erature review on fall detection devices. Their acceptance

Although the studies mentioned earlier cover the current 
state-of-the-art fall detection approaches, most surveys only 
focus on wearable or vision-based fall detection systems. 
Most of the systematic reviews are focused on a specific part 
of fall detection systems, omitting the overall fall detection 
approaches. To address this research gap, we present a liter-
ature study of different fall detection systems following the 
PRISMA research protocol. In this paper, a systematic study 
is performed that provides an overall review of fall-related 
systems categorically. Types of sensors and AI algorithms 
used for existing Fall detection systems are analysed along-
side the success rate of the proposed systems.
If you are new to the field of fall detection, this 
review serves as a structured and practical entry point 
to the domain. We present a comprehensive taxonomy 
of fall detection systems, categorized by sensor modal -
ity—wearable, vision-based, ambient, and multimodal—
and by algorithmic strategy, including threshold-based 
and machine learning approaches. This classification 
framework helps readers grasp the landscape of existing 
approaches, their comparative advantages, and limitations. 
In addition, detailed comparisons of datasets, sensor types, 
and performance metrics are provided to assist research -
ers in selecting appropriate benchmarks and identifying 
research gaps where new contributions can be impactful. 
Unlike prior surveys that narrowly focus on one or two 
modalities or specific algorithm types, this review takes a 
holistic approach by offering a structured taxonomy across 
multiple sensing paradigms and algorithmic layers. This 
enables a critical analysis of cross-modality trade-offs 
and supports informed decision-making for future FDS 
development.
The main contributions of the paper are as follows: 
1. This study covers existing elderly fall detection research 
works published in journals, book chapters, and confer-
ence proceedings between 2006 and 2025.
2. The fall detection approaches are categorised into four 
different types based on implemented sensor types- 
Wearable sensors, Vision sensors, Ambient sensors, 
and Multimodal sensors and subdivided each approach 
based on the implemented algorithms, including thresh-
old-based algorithms and different AI algorithms.
3. Different types of data acquisition sensors that have 
been used in various fall detection works are discussed.
4. It discusses available open-access datasets.
5. The performance of different fall detection approaches 
is critically analysed based on their used methods.
6. Finally, the challenges, limitations of elderly fall detec-
tion, and specific research directions for future enthusi-
astic researchers are given in this paper.

The Accelerometer sensor is the most used one in wear -

eter sensors are also incorporated in many studies to detect 
falls. Furthermore, various sensors, such as ECG, sEMG, 
FSR, etc., are used in wearable Fall detection systems 
(FDS). Apart from wearable sensors, vision sensor-based 
FDS has become popular with computational technology 
advancement in recent years. Depth cameras and RGB 
cameras are the most utilised sensing equipment in vision-

sensor-based FDS has exhibited improved performance and 
earned high acceptance towards the elderly. Ambient sen -
sors like Radio-Frequency Identification (RFID), Acoustic 
sensors, Radar sensors, Pressure sensors, etc., improve the 
FDSs significantly.

Approaches
Research on wearable sensor-based fall detection has gained 
popularity in the research community since 2006. Research-
ers are attempting to detect falls utilising ML and threshold-
based algorithms. Figure 3 represents a general architecture 
of the wearable FDS. This section aims to provide a better

Machine Learning-Based Approaches
Machine learning (ML) methods have significantly trans -
formed wearable Fall Detection Systems (FDS), leveraging 
data from various sensors to improve detection accuracy, 
adaptability, and efficiency. This section synthesizes ML-
based FDS approaches by focusing on sensor types, data 
processing methods, feature extraction, and sensor place -
ment while categorizing similar studies.
Accelerometer and Gyroscope Systems
Accelerometers and gyroscopes are fundamental in ML-
based FDS for capturing motion dynamics. Aziz et al. [ 93] 
utilized a waist-mounted inertial sensor combining a tri-
axial accelerometer and gyroscope to analyze pre-impact 
falls. By examining window sizes and lead times, their 
study demonstrated how SVM classifiers with an RBF 
kernel could achieve accurate fall detection. He et al. [ 75, 
85] employed Kalman filters for estimation and KNN clas -
sifiers to classify falls, optimizing features such as signal 
magnitude and altitude angle. Similarly, Shibuya et al. 
[91] integrated multi-axis accelerometers and gyroscopes 
in a Wireless Gait Analysis Sensor (WGAS), extracting 
six ranges of angular velocity and acceleration features for 
SVM classification. These systems, with sensors placed 
on the upper trunk or waist, emphasize the importance of 
precise feature engineering in robust fall detection. Waheed 
et al. [63] introduced a noise-tolerant fall detection system 
Wearable IMU Systems
Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) combining accelerom -
eters, gyroscopes, and sometimes magnetometers provide 
comprehensive motion analysis. For instance, Liu et al. 
[103] introduced a gravity center detection algorithm with 
IMU (9-axis) data to distinguish falls from normal activities. 
Similarly, Pierleoni et al. [86, 90] developed wearable sys-
tems incorporating Attitude and Heading Reference Systems 
(AHRS) and barometers for improved motion and orientation 
tracking.
Novel Wearable Devices
Innovative FDS designs have emerged, such as footwear-
based systems [ 78], which embed accelerometers and 
force sensors in insoles to analyze pitch, roll, and fall 
indices. Additionally, 9-axis IMU-based systems [ 74, 95] 
focus on angular velocity and trunk motion to detect falls 
and assess risk, demonstrating the versatility of wearable 
FDS.
Camera-Assisted Systems
Camera-integrated wearable systems add a layer of visual 
analysis. Ozcan et al. [96] utilized edge orientation and opti-
cal flow descriptors to detect falls, while their later work 
[80] incorporated HOG and GLBP features for indoor and 
outdoor fall detection.

Multi-Sensor Fusion Systems

ture selection and binary decision tree classifiers to balance 
fall detection accuracy and energy consumption. Nho et al. 
[69] incorporated heart rate sensors with accelerometers, 
leveraging Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) and a two-
stage feature selection process to enhance user adaptabil -
ity. These studies highlight the benefits of sensor fusion in 
improving performance.

Threshold-Based Approaches
Threshold-based approaches have been extensively utilized 
in vision-based Fall Detection Systems (FDS), leveraging 
background subtraction and feature analysis to detect falls. 
These systems often rely on setting optimal threshold values 
for extracted features to distinguish fall events from normal 
activities.
Background Subtraction and Feature Extraction
Rajabi et al. [ 158] proposed a vision-based FDS using a 
Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) for background subtrac -
tion to extract moving objects from video sequences. Fea -
tures such as pixel distance, center of mass width variation, 
self-attention mechanisms to analyze spatial and temporal 
sensor data. This model achieved more than 99% accuracy 
across MobiAct, SisFall, and UniMib-SHAR datasets, effec-
tively distinguishing falls from similar activities. Yu et al. 
[65] employed a TinyCNN in a low-power microcontroller, 
demonstrating 98% sensitivity and specificity on KFall 
and SisFall datasets by capturing both temporal and spatial 
motion features. Medrano et al. [ 79] proposed a novelty 
detection approach using SVM-based anomaly detectors 
to classify fall events as deviations from normal patterns, 
addressing the challenge of diverse fall scenarios. These 
systems exemplify advancements in real-time, resource-
efficient FDS implementations. Guo et al. [ 58] proposed 
PIFall, a wearable fall detection system using high-reso -
lution pressure insoles with a 90-sensor matrix and time-
series "stress video" analysis, employing a ResNet(2+1)D 
model for robust classification of elderly activities.
Innovative Sensor Integration
Integrating novel sensors expands the functionality of FDS. 
Shastry et al. [ 88] combined IMUs with Time of Flight 
(ToF) transceivers to enhance fall detection precision by 
integrating location tracking and reducing false alarms with 
SVM classifiers. Saleh et al. [72] employed low-complexity 
accelerometer-based algorithms, utilizing online feature 
extraction tools to minimize computational burden, and 
applied SVM for accurate fall classification.

3D and Multi-Camera Systems
Leone et al. [ 178] introduced a 3D fall detection system 
using a Time-of-Flight (ToF) camera, where Bayesian seg -

Thresholds on 3D centroid distances effectively detected 
falls, with additional analysis of four distinct human poses. 
Auvinet et al. [177] tackled occlusion challenges using mul-
tiple cameras to compute the Vertical V olume Distribution 
Ratio (VVDR), a feature differentiating lying on the floor 
from other activities. While effective, these methods strug -
gled with scenarios involving furniture interactions.
Foreground-Background Segmentation
Foreground segmentation techniques have also been pivotal. 
Joshi and Nalbalwar [150] developed a system using Gauss-
ian Mixture Models to extract movement data. Features like 
aspect ratio, centroid, orientation, and Hu moments were 
Hu moment invariants, and angle were analyzed, and an 
optimal threshold was applied to identify falls. A finite 
state machine (FSM) was incorporated to minimize detec -
tion time, effectively addressing occlusion and overlapping 
issues.
Similarly, Nguyen et al. [ 153] employed motion history 
and changes in human shape features extracted via Mixture 
of Gaussian (MOG) and Ellipse Fitting techniques. Thresh-
olds were applied to these features to monitor falls from 
pre-fall to post-fall phases. Albawendi et al. [147] extended 
this idea by integrating Time Motion History Image (tMHI) 
analysis and change in human shape orientation, utilizing 
three wall-mounted cameras for feature extraction. Thresh -
olds on motion, shape orientation, and projection histo -
grams enabled efficient fall detection. Wang and Deng [128] 
presented a lightweight and real-time fall detection algo -
rithm combining background subtraction and BlazePose

enhanced efficiency on low-power systems.

Depth Camera-Based Systems
Depth cameras have gained prominence in vision-based 
FDS for their ability to preserve privacy and operate in 
varying lighting conditions. Zhao et al. [ 144] employed a 
depth camera to monitor falls from beds, using Random 
Forest (RF) algorithms to extract human head and upper 
body motion features. The Large Margin Nearest Neighbor 
(LMNN) algorithm was then applied to classify falls versus 
Activities of Daily Living (ADLs).
Hybrid Depth and RGB Systems
Hybrid approaches that combine depth and RGB data 
focus on integrating complementary information from both 
modalities to enhance fall detection. Josemans et al. [ 169] 
demonstrated effective fusion of skeleton-based depth fea -
tures with RGB motion features, showing improved detec -
tion accuracy through Principal Component Analysis (PCA) 
and SVM classification. Bian et al. [ 164] employed an 
infrared depth camera to estimate human key joints using 
a Randomized Decision Tree (RDT) algorithm and used 
these features in conjunction with trajectory analysis for 
fall classification via SVM. These approaches leverage the 
strengths of both depth and RGB data to address challenges 
in dynamic environments.
Single RGB Camera Systems
Single RGB camera systems offer simplicity and cost-effec-
tiveness. Yu et al. [175] developed a semi-unsupervised fall 
detection model using a digital camera positioned near the 
ceiling to track individuals. Background subtraction via 
the codebook method was employed to extract human sil -
houettes, from which features were computed and applied 
to an Online One-Class SVM (OCSVM) for real-time fall 
detection. Two thresholds were introduced to reduce false 
negatives, with the model continuously updated to person -
alize detection for individual users. Building on this, Yu et 
al. [175] applied a similar setup for human posture recog -
nition using a Directed Acyclic Graph SVM (DAGSVM). 
Global and local features, such as histograms and ellipse-
fitting parameters, were extracted and analyzed to classify 
human postures, enabling fall detection through predefined 
conditions. Kaur et al. [132] proposed a hybrid system com-
bining Haar Cascade for human detection and background 
subtraction for motion analysis. Their system demonstrated 
high accuracy on UMAFall and LE2I datasets, address -
ing robustness and efficiency challenges in fall detection 
systems. Mirmahboub et al. [ 171] utilized silhouette area 
changes detected via the Running Average Method and 
integrated these features with an SVM classifier for fall 
thresholded to detect falls in real-time. Similarly, Nghiem et 
al. [174] focused on head detection through modified Histo-
gram of Oriented Gradient (HOG) features to locate heads 
among moving objects, enabling accurate fall identification. 
Edgcomb et al. [ 173] addressed privacy concerns by using 
bounding ovals and Minimum Bounding Rectangles (MBR) 
for feature extraction, employing Dynamic Time Wrap -
ping (DTW) to compute thresholds for fall detection. This 
approach highlighted the importance of temporal feature 
analysis in privacy-sensitive environments.
Low-Cost and Depth-Based Systems
Wahid et al. [ 148] proposed a cost-effective FDS using 
a Pi camera and Raspberry Pi module to reduce system 
complexity. Motion History Images (MHI) and motion 
speed were analyzed using threshold algorithms, though 
human intervention was needed to address false alarms. 
Nizam et al. [ 145] employed a Kinect sensor to calcu -
late fall risk levels, using skeleton tracking and velocity 
thresholds to adapt detection to user-specific risk profiles. 
Depth imaging has also been utilized, as demonstrated by 
Merrouche and Baha [ 154], who extracted human shapes 
from depth frames and analyzed head motion using cova -
riance models. Covariance-based thresholds effectively 
separated falls from normal activities while minimizing 
computational overhead.
Other Threshold-based Vision FDS
Kroputaponchai et al. [ 179] developed a system leverag -
ing Motion Mixture Models to track human shapes across 
frames, examining changes in human shape features to 
identify falls. Kottari et al. [ 140] utilized fisheye cameras 
to capture segmented silhouettes and applied cosine angle-
based thresholds for fall detection, showcasing the effec -
tiveness of low-cost hardware in indoor settings. Fuzzy 
rule-based systems further expanded the scope of threshold-
based approaches. Serrano-Cuerda et al. [ 157] employed 
fuzzy logic to process geometrical and kinematic features 
extracted from segmented human blobs, achieving robust 
detection of falls and associated patterns.

Neural network-based systems leverage multi-layer percep-

using stereo vision sensors to generate 3D representations

using multiple neural networks, demonstrating robustness 
through a voting mechanism. Similarly, Alhimale et al. 
[165] utilized silhouettes normalized from video frames 
as inputs to a neural network, combining FSM models and

Position Embedding, Multi-Head Relation Aggregator, and

Foroughi et al. [ 180] utilised the Integrated Time 
Motion Image (ITMI) and Eigenspace method to obtain 
an efficient elderly FDS which used a neural network for 
fall detection purposes. The temporal motion properties 
of video sequences were extracted using ITMI and then 
projected to Eigenspace to reduce the feature vector. A 
four-layer MLP neural network was applied to detect 
falls and other everyday activities using extracted fea -
tures as input.
Like [180], the temporal motion properties were further 
utilised by Foroughi et al. [ 181] for developing a vision-
based fall detection system. Surveillance cameras were 
implemented to capture video data. Background segmen -
tation was undertaken by quantifying the current frame’s 
difference with a reference frame to obtain a human silhou-
ette. The approximated ellipse fitting method was applied 
to the extracted silhouette to retrieve human shape features. 
The head movement information was extracted from a 
detection. Makantasis and Doulamis [ 168] extended this 
approach by incorporating 3D space approximations from 
monocular camera data, using SVM with a Gaussian Radial 
Basis kernel to classify falls. Harrou et al. [ 151] combined 
Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) with SVM to distin -
guish falls from fall-like activities, leveraging body seg -
mentation into predefined regions for feature extraction.
Multi-Camera Systems
Multi-camera setups enhance system coverage and robust -
ness. Wang et al. [167] proposed a multi-camera FDS using 
surveillance footage, extracting MOSIFT features and 
employing Bag-of-Words representations alongside SVM 
classifiers for fall detection. Hanson et al. [ 163] introduced 
a decentralized approach with intelligent camera networks, 
combining inter-image homographies and Cyclops algo -
rithms for fall detection and localization.
Advanced Feature Extraction and Classification
Innovative algorithms have been developed to address 
computational and accuracy challenges. Ma et al. [ 162] 
utilized Kinect depth cameras to extract Curvature Scale 
Space (CSS) features and applied the Bag-of-Words method 
with an Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) for classifi -
cation. Similarly, Akagündüz et al. [ 156] introduced the 
Silhouette Orientation V olume (SOV) descriptor, employ-
ing K-medoids clustering for feature extraction and Naive 
Bayes for classification. These approaches demonstrated 
efficacy across diverse datasets, including SDU-Fall and 
Weizmann Action.
Skeletal Feature-Based Systems
Recent works have explored skeletal feature-based detec -
tion. Alaoui et al. [142] utilized 2D skeleton models to ana-
lyze key point positions, applying classifiers such as SVM, 
Decision Trees (DT), and Random Forests (RF) to distin -
guish fall incidents. Alazrai et al. [ 160] extended this con -
cept with Kinect sensors to extract Motion Pose Geometric 
Descriptor (MPGD) features from skeletal joints, training 
SVM classifiers for fall event detection.
Silhouette and Privacy-Preserving Methods
Feature extraction remains central to ML-based FDS. Rou -
gier et al. [176] leveraged shape deformation analysis using 
Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) to identify falls through

Performance Analysis and Design Insights into 
Wearable Sensor-Based Fall Detection Systems
Wearable fall detection systems benefit from direct motion 
sensing, enabling high sensitivity to body dynamics through 
accelerometers, gyroscopes, and pressure sensors [ 50, 52, 
104]. Threshold-based methods are lightweight and energy-
efficient but often lack adaptability across individuals, lead-
ing to false alarms or missed detections [ 47, 51]. Machine 
learning approaches, including SVM and KNN, improve 
personalization and pattern recognition [72, 85, 93], though 
their effectiveness can degrade with sensor noise or hard -
ware inconsistency. Deep learning and hybrid models [ 56, 
60, 63] demonstrate robust performance, even under miss -
ing or noisy sensor inputs, but often require greater power 
and memory, limiting use in compact devices. Additionally, 
sensor placement critically impacts performance; wrist or 
ankle-mounted devices may miss certain fall types [ 62, 
94]. While wearable systems are unobtrusive and suitable 
for real-world monitoring, trade-offs exist in accuracy vs. 
generalizability, energy consumption vs. model complexity, 
and sensor fusion vs. hardware cost. Continued research is 
needed on energy-efficient architectures, adaptive thresh -
olds, and multi-sensor fusion for robust and scalable 
deployment.

Performance Analysis and Design Insights into 
Vision-Based Fall Detection Systems
Vision-based fall detection systems offer high detection 
accuracy by leveraging rich spatial and temporal features, 
especially with deep learning models such as CNNs and 
Transformers [ 124, 127, 129, 130]. However, traditional 
threshold-based and ML methods [ 153, 156, 158, 162] 
often lack robustness in complex, cluttered, or low-light 
environments. Deep learning improves generalization but 
introduces computational overhead, requiring large data -
sets and limiting deployment on edge devices. Real-time 
YOLO-based systems [ 126, 131] show promise but strug -
gle in occluded scenes. A persistent limitation across all 
vision-based methods is privacy, particularly in sensitive 
indoor settings, despite efforts using silhouettes or depth 
sensing [ 144, 176]. Overall, while vision approaches 
excel in detailed fall characterization, trade-offs in pri -
vacy, computational cost, and real-world generalizability 
remain key barriers.

Performance Analysis and Design Insights into 
Multimodal Fall Detection Systems
Multimodal fall detection systems integrate wearable, vision, 
and ambient sensors to enhance robustness by leveraging 
complementary strengths across modalities [223, 226, 228]. 
Threshold-based fusion methods, such as those by [224, 227], 
offer low complexity and real-time response but often rely on 
fixed rules, which limits adaptability in dynamic environments. 
Machine learning models [229, 231, 233] achieve high accu-
racy (often over 98%) across various datasets, yet face chal-
lenges with synchronization, increased hardware cost, and data 
heterogeneity. Sensor fusion at the feature or decision level 
improves generalization [235, 244], but may suffer under miss-
ing modality scenarios or sensor failures. Privacy and energy 
constraints also arise, especially in audio-visual and thermal 
systems [230, 232], although edge-based and federated frame-
works mitigate these issues. Overall, while multimodal FDSs 
outperform unimodal systems in terms of accuracy and reli-
ability, practical deployment demands trade-offs in complexity, 
cost, energy efficiency, and privacy preservation.
Existing Challenges and Future Works
There are a number of challenges (see Fig. 8) that need 
to be addressed which are found as research gaps in the 
existing works of fall detection methods. This section 
employed CNN mobileNet model and detected potential fall 
event with an accuracy of 93%. Doukas et al. [246] integrated 
vision and motion sensor data for detecting falls using an 
SVM classifier. The system correctly classified the fall event 
with an accuracy of 100% and .0072 RMS error. Zerrouki 
et al. [ 240] proposed an EWMA-SVM-based fall detection 
model combining accelerometer and vision sensor. Using the 
URFD fall dataset, the proposed EWMA-SVM model out -
performed the most commonly used KNN and SVM classifier 
and achieved an overall accuracy of 96.77%. Tong et al. [239] 
presented a combination of surveillance camera and smart -
phones’ accelerometer-based fall detection system named 
as “FallDetect” using SVM. The system detected fall events 
with an accuracy of above 98%, sensitivity of 99%, specific-
ity of 98%, and outperformed many state-of-the-art models. 
Geertsema et al. [236] developed a fall detection system for 
epilepsy patients using SVM and information fusion of video 
and audio data. This proposed system classified 6 out of 9 
real events of falling down with 99.7% specificity using the 
SEIN dataset and using Le2i dataset, it achieved a sensitiv -
ity of 90% and specificity of 92%. Kepski and Kwolek [241] 
implemented a fall detection scheme embedded with a wear-
able accelerometer and kinect camera. The proposed model 
achieved fall detection accuracy of 99.45% using SVM from 
overhead sequence data of URFD dataset and 95.71% accu-
racy using KNN from a frontal sequence of URFD dataset.
Shin et al. [231] achieved 99.09% accuracy on the UP-Fall 
dataset and 99.32% on UR-Fall by fusing motion-aware skel-
etal features and accelerometer data through a multimodal 
Bi-LSTM-CA and GSTCAN framework. The model showed 
high sensitivity (up to 99.19%) and robust performance even 
in complex fall scenarios, significantly outperforming uni -
modal approaches and prior state-of-the-art systems. Using 
leave-one-group-out validation on the custom UAL-MFDD 
dataset, Lupión et al. [ 232] showed that a multi-branch 
CNN on raw thermal images achieved an F1-score of 0.98; 
IMU-only XGBoost yielded 0.955; and the best fusion of 
raw+OF from all three cameras reached 0.950. The system 
also demonstrated 100% binary fall detection accuracy when

A multimodal FDS incorporating more than one mode 
of sensing elements improves the system’s stability. How -
ever, adding more than one sensing mode to the surveillance 
system may significantly increase the final expense. Differ -
ent sensors may treat similar events differently during the 
monitoring period, leading to false alarms. False alarms also 
occur with some conventional fall detection systems. The 
data analysis process for most multimodal systems involves 
evaluating data for each element individually prior to lever-
aging fusion algorithms to aggregate them. This causes the 
fusion algorithm to be excessively complicated, raising 
computational complexity. As the number of monitoring 
components increases, the more data the system captures, 
and the more computational costs the system incurs.
One of the critical factors affecting fall detection research 
is that of simulated and real fall data. Simulated data offers 
a controlled environment for maintaining consistency and 
repeatability; however, it does not possess the variability 
or unpredictability that might occur in real life. Real fall 
data has invaluable advantages during the validation of per-
formance, but their collection poses quite a few problems, 
starting with ethical ones, up to the difficulty of recording 
authentic falls. Future efforts should prioritize incorporat -
ing real data into analyses while leveraging simulated data 
for initial model development, ensuring both reliability and 
generalizability in practical applications.
Another critical yet under-discussed issue in fall detection 
datasets is subject and environment diversity. Many exist -
ing datasets include a narrow range of participants—often 
healthy, young adults—falling in controlled environments, 
which limits the model’s ability to generalize to real-world 
settings involving elderly individuals, various body types, 
or occluded environments. This bias impacts performance, 
particularly when systems are deployed across populations 
not represented during training. Future research should 
incorporate fairness-aware training protocols, diverse data 
outlines the challenges associated with various fall detec -
tion methods and provides recommendations for further 
research.
Wearable devices’ significant problems are interference, 
uncomfortable device position, unwillingness or uncon -
sciousness to wear, and inconvenience to the users’ mobil -
ity. Unwillingness or unconsciousness to use the gadget may 
lead to an ineffective wearable system. Unwanted sensors 
placed on the human body may lead to pain, discomfort, and 
irritability. Wearable gadgets on the belt around the waist 
cannot be used for getting dressed or undressed and cannot 
be carried whenever a user goes to bed. The inclusion of 
additional sensors contributes to inconvenience and discom-
fort for the user. An approach to solve these problems is to 
encourage the users to select the device position. The device 
can conduct the sensor’s localization on the body to detect 
the device’s location on the user, preventing unnecessary 
placements. Although many falling incidents happen in the 
shower, the users cannot wear a device in the shower as the 
device can be hampered by water and user inconvenience. 
Wearable sensors are battery-powered, which indicates that 
batteries need to be recharged or changed for proper opera -
tion. This issue can be solved by using a low sampling rate 
and hierarchical system approach. It would also reduce 
the system’s computing complexity, thereby minimising 
the computational burden. In their operating time, wear -
able sensors produce significant false alarms, causing user 
annoyance. The rationale for lower accuracy and higher 
false positives when using sensors is a lack of adaptabil -
ity and knowledge of the surroundings. False alarms can be 
minimised by integrating interaction between the user and 
the system. Whenever a fall happens, the user will be first 
alerted to decide if a fall has occurred. Unless the user fails 
to communicate within a predefined time, the emergency 
room will be notified.
Vision-based approaches seem to be case-specific as 
well as dependent on various scenarios. Vision-based algo -
rithms are tested from data obtained from a laboratory set -
ting, experimental conditions including appropriate ambient 
light, normal circumstances or situations, and falls are emu-
lated by recruited participants. Compared to simulated data, 
real-world data challenges are that image quality is poor 
and falling is irregular and differs considerably in terms of 
velocity and type of fall. Their outcomes differ from sce -
nario to scenario. In the low light condition, vision-based 
technologies are inefficient since surveillance cameras fail 
to detect fall events in low light and various lighting states. 
Another significant drawback is the occlusion problem, 
although several approaches claim to solve this issue. The 
vision-based fall detection method often uses complicated 
computer vision techniques to monitor a person, requir -

An important challenge in the research on fall detection 
is related to methodologies employed in validation. The 
vast majority of works are based on internal cross-valida -
tion, which relies on random splits of available data into 
training and test sets. While it does evaluate model perfor -
mance, it mostly lacks the generalization capability to new, 
previously unseen scenarios like different users or differ -
ent environments. External validation techniques, such as 
leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, could give a more 
realistic estimation of performance by training a model on 
completely new subjects to simulate real-world scenarios. 
Future research is encouraged to adopt external validation 
techniques for better assessment of the strength and gen -
eralization of fall detection systems under varying settings 
and populations.
Real-time fall detection technologies are highly rel -
evant to ensure immediate intervention and safety for the 
user. Systems using this technology must resolve issues of 
latency minimization, computational efficiency optimiza -
tion, and feasibility of implementation in diverse settings. 
Although a few solutions already depict their potential, most 
of them suffer from the trade-off between speed and accu -
racy, particularly in resource-constrained devices. Future 
research must be directed to improve real-time algorithms 
with a reduction in system complexity, ensuring robust per-
formance in dynamic settings, which would make fall detec-
tion scalable and effective.
Conclusion
Elderly fall is the second leading cause of death among all 
injuries. It causes great effects on the elderly independent 
living as there is no one to rescue the older adults during 
falls immediately. Many studies have been developed to date 
to detect and predict fall incidents at the earliest time. This 
study presented the existing fall detection approaches based 
on artificial intelligence algorithms as well as the sensors 
they have utilised. A systematic literature search has been 
performed in popular databases such as Scopus, PubMed, 
Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore. More than 145 research 
articles have been chosen carefully as per PRISMA guide -
lines. We have categorised the fall detection approaches into 
four different types, including wearable sensors, vision sen-
sors, ambient sensors, and multi-modal sensor-based sys -
tems. Furthermore, we have subdivided each fall detection 
approach into different categories based on the methods they 
used, including threshold-based algorithms and different AI 
algorithms. The publicly available datasets were discussed.
