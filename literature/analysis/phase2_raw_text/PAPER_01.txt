ABSTRACT Falls are the leading cause of injuries and fatalities among older individuals propagating
concerns for health and safety. Mitigating these concerns requires timely intervention and response to
minimize health complications. Autonomous fall detection systems are often offered as a means to alleviate
these concerns. Typical fall detection systems classify a fall event using either inertial- or vision-based data.
Aside from these two modes of input for fall detection, sparse human fall audio data is available. Audio
datasets associated with the dynamic impact of human falls from the perspective of a spatial environment are
both unavailable publicly and focused on niche applications. This dataset provides multimodal composition
of visual and audio data to aid in fall detection technique development. Though this dataset emphasizes audio
novelty, a synchronized visual component is included offering a multifaceted collection of data. Audio input
for fall detection systems presents an opportunity for new approaches towards autonomous fall detection.
This dataset comprises human movement representing activities of daily living and falls of 25 participants
in various residential settings, yielding 1,300 instances of unique visual and audio samples. Promising
utility of the bi-modal multiperspective percussive (BiMP) dataset is demonstrated through experimental
data evaluations using techniques including: GoogLeNet, Long Short Term Memory, Continuous Wavelet
Transforms, and Short-time Fourier Transforms for human fall detection achieving accuracies up to 96%.
The concept of audio-based fall detection has the potential to mitigate concerns regarding privacy and
invasiveness, in addition to broadening the scope of fall detection mechanisms.
INDEX TERMS Assisted living, audio analysis, fall detection, machine learning, multimodal dataset.

Falls in older adults are a significant public health issue
with profound effects on independence, quality of life,
and overall healthcare costs [1]. Falls being the leading
cause of injuries and injury-related deaths among older
adults [2], [3], increases demand for effective fall detection
and prevention (FDP) support mechanisms. Physical impacts
of falling cause complications for older adults who wish to

From a high-level perspective, most FDP systems share
a general mode of operation, involving input data from
real-time monitoring and processing the data through a
classifier. V ariations of FDP schemes differ in aspects such

Aside from visual- and sensor-based approaches towards
FDP systems development, with exceptions such as [9]
and [10], the potential for audio fall detection has been largely
overlooked. Both existing audio-based FDPs and the utilities
necessary for their development are scarce. Notwithstanding
the resource of already existent and not publicly available
datasets, i.e., [9] and [10], audio data from falls is not
commonly recognized for or preserved as a viable means of
input for fall detection mechanisms. Characterisation of falls
in terms of audio information has the potential to invoke more
avenues of autonomous fall detection.
Audio fall detection, as presented in scenarios such as [9],
provide visual-audio recordings inclusive of verbal responses
of older adults during falls. Similarly, [10] perpetuates falling
audio for: verbal response, body collision, and background
noise using wearable cameras for data collection. These
efforts provide a benchmark for application of audio fall
detection, establishing nuance within the FDP context.
Furthering audio fall detection, this work aims to provide fall
audio independent of verbal utterance from an environmental
perspective. The focus is to capture the dynamic impact audio
of a fall from a listening device in proximity of a person,
as opposed to being attached. Although verbal interference
from a fallen person merits consideration, so too do fall
instances when no verbal response is able to be provided by
a fallen individual.
In this paper, a multimodal and multiperspective human
fall dataset with synchronized audio and visual formats from a
spatial environmental perspective is developed and evaluated
using deep learning and data processing techniques. This
dataset places emphasis on the collection and inclusion of
dynamic impact fall audio. Further, this dataset aims to
address the lack of publicly available audio fall impact
data independent of verbal input from an environmental
perspective, capturing the audio essence of a human fall
as accurately as possible. Recognizing the utility of video
data in the context of autonomous fall detection, the audio
data presented is accompanied by complimentary video data.
Inclusion of both audio and visual data offers the capacity for
multimodal analysis and evaluation of fall detection methods,
opposed to analyses using a singular visual- or audio-
input. To evaluate the developed bi-modal multiperspective
percussive (BiMP) dataset, this paper devises multiple FDP
methods, using deep learning as well as image and audio
processing techniques, for fall classification. The devised
FDP methods not only demonstrate the potential of the BiMP
dataset for FDP system development and evaluation, but also
prove to be effective FDP methods for fall detection and
classification.
Fall detection systems can be trained using this dataset
and implemented in environments with expected older adult
ambulation. This dataset provides a means to expand the
utility of smart home and mobile robotic devices, thus
promoting safety for older adults. For example, fall detection
systems can be integrated into one or more of the many smart
home devices that are already present in the homes of older
adults, which capture audio or visual data.
This paper includes a review of work related to visual,
sensorial, and audio fall detection mechanisms, along with
a review of available human fall datasets (Section II),
followed by the methodology of this dataset’s construction
(Section III). The methodology discusses the breakdown
of participants, environments, equipment used, and fall
characteristics exhibited in this dataset. It also includes
the demographic statistics and physical characteristics of
participants, environmental considerations, and fall simula-
tion instructions. Furthermore, the storage and processing
of data is discussed to articulate the hierarchy of data
storage, along with precautions taken to ensure participant
privacy (Section IV). Additional analysis (Section V), results
(Section VI), limitations (Section VI-B), and conclusions
(Section VII) are discussed providing context towards the
viability of this dataset for FDP system development and eval-
uation. This work was conducted under Clarkson University’s
Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval number 24-06.
II. RELATED WORK
The following sections are intended to articulate some
relevant FDP systems and the methods on which they rely.
The first section discusses available FDPs that rely on
video input and the techniques leading to fall classification.
Similarly, the following section discusses FDPs reliant on
sensor-based data, and the methods for classification used.
The section following discusses methods used to classify
a fall based on audio input, while the final section offers
insights on already available fall datasets.
A. VISUAL-BASED FDP SYSTEMS
Fall detection using visual inputs implement various tech-
niques of image processing. Conventional practices in the
analysis of visual data can be typically achieved through
image manipulation or using key point locations, examples
of which can be found in [11]. Examples of image manipu-
lation would include masking, augmentation, or background
extraction. Alternatively, methods using key point locations
typically define a systematic classification of joint locations
or features. Analyses using key points typically result in

Image manipulation can be seen in the method presented
in [12] to detect falls using human body shape. Exemplifying
the usage of background removal, this technique extracted
background information from each video frame and used
the leftover foreground information in the determination of a
fall. Similarly, background segmentation in [13] was accom-

binary images using a mask region-based convolutional
neural network (R-CNN). This study used both a publicly
available dataset University of Rzeszów (UR) fall detection
dataset [14] in conjunction with a self-collected dataset.
Fall detection was achieved using secondary convolutional
neural network (CNN) feature extractions as inputs of
an attention guided bi-directional long short-term memory
(LSTM) classifier. Using a specified human posture model
derived from joint configurations, [15] extrapolated kinetic
information of a person in an RGB video as input to a logistic
regression classifier for fall determination. Additionally,
[15] constructed a custom dataset in combination with the
FallVideo dataset from [16]. Further examples of visual fall
detection were presented in [17] using image manipulation
and key joint locations.
Despite the application of work done previously, limi-
tations include classification bias due to low variation of
data, and requirements for continuous visual monitoring.
Nonetheless, available work involving visual inputs for fall
detection demonstrate the utility and application of visual fall
datasets. Additional to the datasets used to develop existing
visual fall detection techniques, a higher volume of visual
fall data, from alternative viewpoints offers a more diverse
sample set on which to train fall detection mechanisms. Fall
data from multiple perspectives effectively facilitates a wider
range of view angles from which a fall can be detected.
B. SENSOR-BASED FDP SYSTEMS
Sensor-based fall detection involves a vast selection of
sensors. Some sensors are intended to be worn, and others
are intended to be integrated into an environment, such as
floors. These include, but are not limited to, sensors such
as: pressure plates, accelerometers, gyroscopes, manometers,
and inertial measurement units. Sensor-based fall detection
can be accomplished using a single type of sensor, or a
combination of multiple sensors in a multi-input mode of
detection.
For instance, [18] utilized data from a smartphone
accelerometer in combination with a threshold-based method
to detect different types of events, e.g., a fall-like event,
such as lying on the floor, or a sudden stop after walking.
If a fall-like event was detected by the threshold-based
method, a multiple kernel learning support vector machine
was implemented to confirm a fall, or a nonfall. Similarly,
an inertial measurement unit combining an accelerometer,
gyroscope, and magnetometer were used in [19]. Using the
data from these devices, an evaluation was then carried
out for classifications using: a threshold based method,
a threshold based method with Madgwick’s Decomposition,
and machine learning methods. Using a piezoelectric sensor,
[20] developed a fall detection system based on floor
vibration characteristics.
The implementation of sensors has shown to be a viable
option towards a solution for FDP mechanisms. However,
limitations are inherent due to the invasive nature of wearable
sensors, in addition to sparsity of available training data
for niche sensor configurations. To this end, despite the
comparability of input data with that of visual methods,
sensor-based datasets offer insight and methodologies that
are applicable in the construction of a fall dataset in spite of
dissimilar fall data format.
C. AUDIO-BASED FDP SYSTEMS AND DATASETS
There are very few datasets and FDP systems that focus
on audio-based fall detection. In [9], visual and audio fall
detection methods were developed using a self-procured
dataset inclusive of phonetic audio associated with a fall,
in addition to visual fall data. The visual fall detection
used a histogram-based classification of silhouette extraction
and postural recognition, combined with an audible call for
help. Similarly, [10] used visual data, combined with verbal,
and environmental audio from egocentric cameras. The
method was evaluated using decision fusion techniques for
various visual learning models, and Mel-frequency Cepstral
Coefficient audio features.
These techniques leverage fall associated audio for fall
detection although verbal utterances accompanying a fall
are a prevalent constituent in the analyses. Despite the
distinguished advantage of audio in FDP techniques pre-
sented by these works, further investigation should address
instances of falls resulting in, or causing unconsciousness,
incoherence, or compromised speech [28]. These instances
are in correlation with limitations inherent in audio FDP
systems, and audio fall data availability. However, these
further steps would require the development of a new fall
dataset that includes the required audio data, since such a
dataset is not publicly available.
Impact audio is an inherent constituent in falls of conscious
and unconscious persons alike. An FDP designed to detect
impact audio has the potential to recognize a fall in events
where a person is unresponsive. The sparse nature of dynamic
impact audio of falls prompts the development of a resource
inclusive of such. Impact audio data would thus provide
means for nuanced autonomous fall detection.
The presented work is aimed towards providing a resource
effectively capturing diverse fall data enabling development
of both visual and audio FDP techniques. The visual
portion of this work is intended to provide a comprehensive

D. HUMAN FALL DATASETS
Human fall datasets (HFDs) are a significant contribution
to the development of learning-based fall detection meth-
ods [29]. Fall datasets typically consist of orchestrated falls,
which safely produce data that imitates characteristics of an
actual fall. Typical constituents of HFDs include fall events
(FEs), which exemplify fall characteristics, and activities
of daily living (ADLs), which provide characteristics of
regularly anticipated movement.
Established HFDs are listed in Table 1. There are
commonalities shared between each of the listed datasets,
although they present human fall data in different capacities.
Each dataset interprets fall dynamics from distinct reference
frames, capturing and quantifying signals produced by the
motion. The presented datasets capture unique signals using
various methodologies, and sensing equipment.
Datasets such as in CIRDO Corpus [9] and MUVIM [26]
employ methodologies depicting ADLs and FEs for scenario-
based recording. In contrast, isolated instance-based record-
ing of FEs and ADLs separately is typical of the other datasets
presented.
In [21], the SisFall dataset is presented, which is comprised
of acceleration and rotation data using two accelerometers
and a gyroscope from thirty-eight participants. The sensors
were placed at the waist of each participant. Additional
preliminary analyses were included to test the dataset,
using fourteen extracted features and two threshold-based
classifiers. Activities were performed in classrooms using a
landing mat to ensure participant safety.
Similarly, [22] presents a motion signal dataset, referred
to as FallAllD, as a result of an accelerometer, gyroscope,
magnetometer, and barometer readings placed at waist, wrist,
and neck positions, for numerous movements performed
by fifteen participants. Acceleration-based fall classification
was conducted using deep learning and machine learning
techniques for the FallAllD dataset. Participants performed
movements using protective gear including a helmet, arm
protection, knee pads, and a back protector, eliminating the
need for a dampening fall pad to minimize data interference.
The MobiFall dataset in [23], presents smartphone
accelerometer and gyroscope data collection for twenty-four
participants. The dataset was recorded using smartphones
providing data for two primary approaches to fall detection:
threshold-based and machine learning methods. The smart-
phone was placed naturally in a pocket of the participant
during recording of movements. Additionally, a landing mat
was used to protect participants while performing FEs.
The TST Fall detection dataset presented in [24], provides
RGB-D visual and acceleration data from two accelerome-
ters. Classification of visually determined joint positions and
accelerometer angle and acceleration from eleven volunteers
were used to produce a dataset evaluating combined visual-
and sensor-based fall detection. Analyses highlight the
benefit of data synchronization between sensors. Data was
recorded using a singular depth camera, in addition to two
wrist and a waist mounted IMUs for eleven participants.
Using a combination of a smartphone, accelerometer, gyro-
scope, and magnetometer, the UMAFall dataset[25] provides
sensor data from seventeen participants for five sensor loca-
tions. Comparative acceleration magnitudes for individual
sensing points were presented for classification. Sensors
were attached to the ankle, waist, right wrist, chest, and a
smartphone was carried in the right pocket. Three unique
environments were used to collect data, implementing mat-
tresses to prevent participant injury while performing falls.
The MUVIM visual dataset was collected in [26], pro-
viding RGB, infrared, depth, and thermal image data. All
cameras were mounted to the ceiling and were used to
record forty participants. Visual data for each camera was
comparatively evaluated using a 3D CNN auto-encoder. The
study included scenarios that contained both scripted ADLs
and FEs. Not all recordings were inclusive of both ADLs
and FEs, some only simulated ADLs. Furthermore, the safety
equipment used was a crash mat to cushion the fall of
participants performing FEs.

in [10], used RGB and IR cameras to record fourteen
participants. Visual classification was conducted using four
learning models combined with features extracted from the
audio using Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC).
Cameras were placed egocentrically to collect RGB, infrared,
and audio data. Classification schemes used late decision
fusion, integrating descriptors from visual and auditory
data. The audio data included ambient and verbal sounds
surrounding the event. The egocentric camera positions were
on the neck and waist of fourteen participants.
The BiMP dataset is uniquely intended to present multi-
modal (visal and audio) representations of FEs and ADLs
from a diverse set of viewing angles and perspectives. Addi-
tionally, the audio data provided in the BiMP dataset captures
the fall impact noise independent of vocal interjection.
The ADLs and FEs used in the BiMP dataset were
selected with reference to those used in previous datasets.
Additionally, the modalities of the BiMP dataset are evaluated
independent of one another to establish the fall characteristics
inherent in each modality. The following methodology pro-
vides a comprehensive explanation of constituent attributes
of the BiMP dataset.
III. METHODOLOGY
Effective learning-based fall detection relies on a dataset that
accurately aligns with the intended application, is diverse
in composition, and provides sufficient volume to allow for
performance evaluations [30]. Thus, this dataset is purposed
towards a robust and cohesive presentation of data, reflective
of multiple anticipated real-world scenarios.
Accurate recreation of an anticipated scenario demands
consideration and minimization of variation between antic-
ipated and recorded events. This section provides the
background information, rationale, and the framework for the
various aspects involved in the construction of the fall dataset.
To achieve a fall dataset comprised of accurate, multi-
perspective visual and audio data, it is important to clearly
formulate the required constituents defining these qualities.
Therefore, the methodology for data collection must consider
factors affecting the desired fall data. These factors include:
the particular fall characteristics, the activities of the study
and movement definitions, the environment in which the
collection happens, participant demographics and character-
istics, and the equipment used to record the data.
The following sections provide further insight around the
factors listed above, beginning with the fall characteristics
inherent in the target demographic of older adults.
A. FALL CHARACTERISTICS
Accurate data collection requires the integration of innate
characteristics associated with falls of older adults in order
to reproduce similar motions. The nature of falls in older
individuals according to [31], concluded that the most
prominent culprit responsible for falls was due to improper
shift of the individual’s weight. Furthermore, investigation
in [32], indicated that longer choice step reaction time
(CSRT) resulted in higher risk of falling. The relationship
drawn between aging and longer CSRT implied older adults
subject to falling would also be typically subject to a limited
reaction time, thus, resulting in a fall where reactionary
reflexes lag behind. This results in a fall where the impact
is not cushioned by appendages or limbs in a manner that
a person with a typical reaction time would be able to
mitigate the severity of the impact associated with a fall.
Similarly, [33] offered support for the idea that impaired
reaction time placed older individuals at higher risk of injury
in the event of a fall.
Not all older adults are subject to prolonged reaction times
or higher impact severity. Some older adults are able to
cushion their falls in a timely manner; however, it is not
common. In [34], reaction times in arm responses between a
younger demographic and an older demographic during a fall
were observed. The findings of this study support the idea that
reaction time is prolonged with an age increase. This paper
also indicated that the reaction of older individuals, in terms
of physical response to a fall, is to use their arms to dampen
their fall.
Aside from a lower capacity for optimal reaction time,
there is little indication that older adults are not inclined to
reactionary measures of using their hands to cushion a fall,
albeit with a delayed response time. The reflexive instinct
for a person to use their hands and arms to dampen a fall
transcends the age differential and remains a prevalent mode
of fall mitigation among older adults.

C. ENVIRONMENT
The previous section addressed procedural variables asso-
ciated with bodily motion, while this section addresses the
environmental considerations.
Background information can significantly influence learn-
ing networks as it can affect attributes such as training bias,
context, and feature extraction. Both [35] and [36] present
examples of how image background impacts the ability of
the learning network. Although these works are not directly
concerned with the development of human fall datasets, they
communicate the influence background information can have
on learning networks. The significance of image background
in the context of learning, demonstrates the importance of
data collection in an environment that conveys the visual
ambience of the intended application.
Visual background information included in this dataset
was captured in a mock home laboratory. The setting was
chosen for the relevance it provides within the context of this
work, as it replicates an environment where older adults are
statistically prone to fall [3].
The laboratory rooms used in the data collection are:
(a) living space, (b) bedroom, (c) kitchen/dining room,
(d) laundry room, and (e) secondary bedroom. Due to the
relatively low percentage of falls that actually occur within
the bathroom [37], and privacy implications, a bathroom
setting was not included in this data collection. Additionally,
outdoor environments have been omitted from data collection
as this work is aimed towards further development of
aging in place technologies. Concern is therefore prioritized
within the home rather than the external environment.
It is worth noting that in addition to privacy challenges
related to the placement of fixed cameras in bathrooms,
bathrooms are often constrained spaces with unique elements
dissimilar to the remainder of the home (e.g., obscuring
curtains, smooth tubs, wet features, locking doors), which
can make it difficult for mobile robots to access or observe.
Similarly, fixed cameras are typically impractical in most
outdoor environments, and outdoor environments can have
challenging geographic features, poor lighting conditions,
and inclement weather that restrict the use of mobile robots
for data capture.

performed, are Clarkson University’s Simulation Laboratory.

E. EQUIPMENT
Data was collected with a Reolink RLK8-800B4 sys-
tem [39], comprised of four 3840p×2160p, 20 frames per
second cameras, combined with a two terabyte network
video recorder. Cameras were placed as evenly around the
room as possible, each of which viewed the same central
location.
The positioning of the cameras in this fashion offered
multiple viewing angles of the same incident, while also
recording audio data from diverse spatial regions. In each
room, cameras were placed as evenly as possible around
the incident to avoid data similarity. The vertical place-
ment of each camera in this study ranged from 90 cm
to 180 cm. While not specifically designed for mobile
humanoid robots, this range of heights is well-suited for the
training and testing of FDP techniques in mobile robotic
systems [40].
The equipment used for safety included a vest for torso
padding, a helmet, knee and elbow pads, and gloves. Safety
equipment used for the fall events was worn as shown in
Fig. 2. The knee and elbow pads were equipped with adhesive
cloth to imitate clothing. The pads were covered in cloth
in order to provide audio mimicking the impact sound of a
clothed joint opposed to that of a safety pad.
Each participant wore this safety equipment while per-
forming all FEs. Similar to the unwanted interference landing
mats play in accelerometer data collection [22], audio data
collection is subject to the same influence. To avoid any
potential interference from the landing mat on the integrity
of data collection, this study opted to use wearable safety
equipment.
Comprehensive inclusion of the the spatial, movement,
and participant attributes provide context for the recorded
data, which was then processed and reposited for further
analysis.

V. ANALYSIS
This analysis serves to evaluate both the visual and audio
dataset components for fall detection. Both audio and visual
constituents were independently evaluated using unique and
distinguishable class features. Thus, classification techniques
were employed for audio and visual data independently in
order to quantify the recognizable fall characteristics inherent
in each modality. The dataset was used to train and test
classifiers for both data modalities, purposed towards binary
classification of FEs as falls and ADLs as nonfalls.
This work prioritized the classification of falls over
specific movement classifications, thereby removing the
necessity of a non-binary classification scheme. More
specific movement classification beyond fall or nonfall is
beyond the scope of this research, but is achievable using this
dataset.

The architecture of the inception modules leverages dif-

Each inception module provides multi-level feature extrac-
tion, where medium-sized spatial features are captured by
the (3 × 3) convolutions and larger spatial features are
captured by the (5 × 5) convolutions. These inception
modules are sequentially connected in between max pooling
layers, creating inception sequences (IS) for further feature
extraction.
The inception sequence of tandem inception modules,
as shown in Fig. 4, are labeled according to the number
appearing next to the module. The sequences are structured
as follows:
• IS 1: 2 sequential inception modules
• IS 2: 5 sequential inception modules
• IS 3: 2 sequential inception modules
Each inception sequence captures information at different
levels as a result of combined outputs of elements from the
processing layers of the module. The first inception sequence
extracts basic low-level features. The initial sequence focuses
on finer details in the feature extraction, such as edges
colors and textures. The second inception sequence is
responsible for intermediate features, capturing more context
than the preceding sequence, such as shapes or objects. The
subsequent and final inception sequence captures the highest-
level, most abstract features, representing patterns or objects
of higher complexity.

C. AUDIO COMPONENT CLASSIFIERS
The classification of the audio portion of this dataset used
the same GoogLeNet architecture displayed in Fig. 4. The
GoogLeNet architecture accepts images for classification,
thus, audio data was formatted as pictorial inputs. Raw audio
signals were extracted from each video and were processed
without filtering. The exploration of filtering processes
extends beyond the focus of this work and provides a basis for
future analyses that may prove to be advantageous regarding
data clarity or classification performance. The experiments
were conducted in closed environments focused on the
noise generated by the activity. It was expected that sounds
generated by the participants performing the activities would
contribute to the background noise. However, separation of
mixed audio signals produced by concurrent sources is not
a trivial task [42], and deviated beyond the scope of this
work. Depending on the intended application, the addition
or removal of background noise from the dataset may be
necessary to improve fall detection outcomes. The noise
present in the current dataset can be considered reflective of a
quiet home environment. Similarly, filtering techniques may
be considered in future work as none have been included as
part of the analyses in this work.
Audio signals were converted into images through the
application of wavelet and spectrogram transforms, capturing
the temporal data within a single image. The transforms
return the frequency and amplitude constituents of an audio
signal over time. Frequency quantifies the amount of repeated
vibrations captured in a signal over a given period of time.
The amplitude of a signal, is the magnitude of the vibration
experienced at a given time or frequency. Short-time Fourier
transforms and continuous wavelet transforms were chosen
for their unique abilities to produce time-frequency detailed
representations of signal characteristics as a learnable
image [43].

B. LIMITATIONS
The limited diversity of participant characteristics in the
dataset is a current limitation. While participant demo-
graphics include various characteristics, the participants in
the study should ideally include older adults exclusively.
However, this is a known challenge due to the inher-
ent safety issues involved in older adult simulation of
fall events. Similarly, gender and ethnicity should ideally
reflect a comparative composition of the senior population;
whereas, the study demographics more closely reflect the
geographic region wherein the dataset was developed,
namely, rural, upstate New Y ork, USA. In this dataset,
the geographical limitations inherent to the area wherein
this study was performed, namely, rural upstate New Y ork,
bias the demographics to those of the local region. The
application of this dataset should consider both the specific
target demographic, and for widespread implementations, the
broader population diversity and characteristics. However,
demographic inaccuracies are inherent for any instance
of training data noninclusive of the anticipated user(s)
exclusively. Future work could consider collecting data from
constant observation of older adults in their living settings
to increase the fidelity of the dataset. However, aside from
increased cost, observation duration, and privacy concerns,
this also creates challenges related to both capturing multiple
perspectives and variety in the characteristics of the falls
observed.
The impact of dissimilarities present in the BiMP dataset
participant and intended user demographics are not easily
quantifiable, but have the capacity to influence classification
metrics of various system architectures. The impact of
demographic diversity on the widespread applicability of the
models used is beyond the scope of this work. The models
used in this analysis aid in the evaluation of the ability of
the dataset to provide data detectable by various methods.
The models are not intended for widespread replication and
implementation, but rather to serve as a benchmark for the
contents of the dataset.
Emphasis has been placed on the background used for
data collection. While environmental variables are intended
to replicate those characteristic of a home, it is impossible to
account for all the application environments.
The safety equipment used was not typical attire of older
adults prone to falling. The inclusion of safety equipment
negatively affects the similarity of collected data and unantic-
ipated falls. The safety equipment is a necessary precaution;
however, its implementation produces the potential for
unwanted elements affecting signal frequency of audio data,
and visually inaccurate attire. Nonetheless, participant safety
is paramount to any other aspect of this work.
Limitations are also inherent in the inability for orches-
trated falls to perfectly display characteristics of a natural fall.
Orchestrated and natural falls display different reactionary
elements. The reflex response in each of these cases
exemplify dissimilar body weight shifts and response times.
These differences potentially influence visual data, and
the impact responsible for the audio signal; nonetheless,
minimization of these concerns was achieved by instructing
participants to slightly delay their reflexes while falling.
VII. CONCLUSION
This dataset presents multiple perspectives of a singular
incident, providing unrepeated activity recordings. Further,
multi-perspective recording captures unique environmental
fall audio characteristics, providing a basis for sound-based
fall detection from varying listening locations.
Data processing using GoogLeNet singularly and with an
additional LSTM showed promising results as both systems
displayed aptitude for differentiating between falls and non-
falls with accuracies ranging from 91%-96%. Much like the
results from the visual analysis, CWT and STFT processing
techniques used for audio fall detection exhibited accuracies
ranging between 89% and 96% ability in distinguishing fallen
and nonfallen states.

A foreseeable utility of this dataset leverages the preva-

provide new potential avenues for audio fall detection using
smart home devices. The existent listening nature of virtual
assistants and smart home devices within households provide
a secure means for audio monitoring and therefore fall

confirmation using visual data during or after the incident;
having applications in environments with any combination
of listening or visual monitoring capabilities, including smart
homes. Additionally the efficacy of weighted voting is being
explored when using both input modalities. Further learning
methods could incorporate aggregation or decision level
fusion of individual classifiers.
Considerations for potential challenges regarding signal
interference from additional sources are beyond the scope
of this work. The reasoning behind these exclusions stems
from the focus of this work to provide a basis to be
built upon, meaning to capture the essence of the incident
without it being convoluted with interfering signals. Thus,
providing adequate basis for scenarios noninclusive of
outside interference while simultaneously allowing the ability
to add interferences specific to the objectives of further work.
The novelty of this dataset is highlighted in the presentation
of variable perspective, multi-modal (e.g., visual and audio)
fall data. Broad analyses leveraging image processing, signal

for the efficacy of the proposed dataset. Promising classi-
