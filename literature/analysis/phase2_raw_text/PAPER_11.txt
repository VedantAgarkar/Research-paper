ABSTRACT
Falls among older adults represent a serious public health problem, particularly for individuals living alone or in 
assisted-care environments, where delayed intervention can lead to severe injuries or death. Although numerous 
fall-detection systems have been proposed, many existing approaches suffer from limitations, including high 
computational complexity, limited interpretability, reliance on centralized video storage, and reduced general-
ization across diverse environments. These challenges motivate the development of lightweight, explainable, and 
trustworthy fall-detection framework for healthcare applications. To address these limitations, this paper pro-
poses a vision-based fall detection framework that integrates MobileNetV2 for efficient spatial feature extraction 
and a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) for temporal motion modeling. In addition, a federated learning strategy is 
employed to support collaborative training across distributed data sources without requiring centralized 
collection of raw video recordings, thereby reducing reliance on centralized storage while enabling learning from 
heterogeneous environments. During deployment, a sliding-window inference strategy is adopted to achieve 
reliable and low-latency fall detection. Model transparency is further supported through Grad-CAM++ visual 
explanations, while Fairlearn is used to assess performance fairness across different fall types and user groups. 
Experimental results on the combined CAUCAFall and Le2i datasets demonstrate that the proposed framework 
achieves an accuracy of 97.14%, a precision of 99.26%, a recall of 96.75%, and an F1-score of 97.99%. Although 
the achieved accuracy is 2.46 percentage points lower than the highest reported accuracy (99.60%), the 
competing method employs a substantially more complex multi-stage architecture. In contrast, the proposed 
framework adopts a lightweight MobileNetV2–GRU architecture while integrating explainability through Grad- 
CAM++, fairness evaluation using Fairlearn, and decentralized collaborative learning. Furthermore, the pro-
posed framework achieves an inference throughput of approximately 93 FPS on the experimental platform used 
in this study, demonstrating high computational efficiency.
1. Introduction
Falls among older adults represent a serious public health concern, 
especially for elderly individuals who live alone or reside in assisted- 
care and nursing facilities. Many older adults prefer to remain inde-
pendent by living in their own homes, while others rely on institutional 
care due to age-related health conditions. In both situations, regular 
monitoring is crucial, as older adults are more susceptible to falls and 
related injuries. However, providing constant manual supervision for 
every individual is often impractical, costly, and time-consuming, 
particularly in long-term care settings. When assistance is delayed 
after a fall, the risk of severe injury, long-term disability, or death in-
creases significantly. This issue is further compounded by the high 
frequency of falls among older adults worldwide, making it not only an 
individual safety concern but also a major public health problem 
(Giovannini et al., 2022). Falls are common and recurring events in this 
age group and are recognized as the sixth leading cause of morbidity and 
mortality among older adults (Chen et al., 2025; El Sayed et al., 2023). 
According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 500,000 
fatal falls occur globally each year, most of which involve individuals 
aged 65 years and older (World Health Organization, 2021). Addition-
ally, nearly one-third of older adults experience at least one fall each 
year, and about 10% of these incidents result in serious injuries, such as 
fractures or intracranial trauma (Kakara et al., 2025). These statistics 
underscore the significant health burden posed by falls among the 
elderly population (Kalache et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2025).

Given the serious impact of falls on the health and safety of older 
adults, rapid detection and assistance are essential to reduce the risk of 
severe injuries. Automated monitoring systems provide a practical so-
lution by continuously observing individuals, and promptly sending 
alerts to family members or nursing staff in case of detecting fall in-
cidents. This rapid response can significantly improve outcomes by 
ensuring that help is provided as quickly as possible. To achieve this 
goal, researchers have developed various fall-detection approaches. 
Some methods rely on wearable sensors (Yao et al., 2026), while others 
use vision-based techniques to monitor individuals and enhance their 
safety without requiring constant human supervision (Apicella & 
Snidaro, 2021; Batumalai et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025). Wearable 
sensor methods, particularly those employing inertial measurement 
units (IMUs), have attracted considerable research interest due to their 
compact size, low cost, and high detection accuracy (Krupitzer et al., 
2019). Despite these advantages, their adoption among older adults 
remains limited, as such devices often cause discomfort and require 
frequent recharging of batteries, which can negatively affect user 
compliance. Moreover, wearable sensors are susceptible to various 
sources of noise, including improper device placement and dynamic 
interactions between the body and the sensor, which can lead to 
increased false alarm rates. In addition, these systems are often associ-
ated with higher installation and maintenance costs and are sensitive to 
environmental conditions and interference (Fei et al., 2023; 
Wickramasinghe et al., 2017).
By contrast, computer vision–based fall-detection methods overcome 
many of the limitations of wearable systems by enabling non-intrusive 
monitoring without requiring older adults to wear specialized devices. 
By analyzing video data, these approaches can capture rich contextual 
information about body posture and movement, which supports more 
reliable fall detection in real-world settings (Yu et al., 2025). For this 
reason, video-based non-contact fall detection has attracted increasing 
attention as an effective alternative, offering continuous real-time 
monitoring without active user involvement (Chen et al., 2024; Huang 
et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Newaz & Hanada, 2024; Newaz et al., 
2023; Yang et al., 2024). This is particularly important for older 
adults with cognitive impairments, such as dementia, for whom wearing 
and maintaining sensor-based devices may be difficult. In addition, the 
widespread availability of cameras allows vision-based systems to take 
advantage of existing surveillance infrastructure, enabling scalable 
deployment not only in smart homes but also in hospitals, nursing fa-
cilities, and other public or institutional environments. At the same time, 
recent advances in deep learning have significantly improved the ac-
curacy of vision-based models by enhancing their ability to learn 
spatiotemporal patterns from video data. As a result, the performance 
gap between camera-based and sensor-based fall-detection systems have 
been substantially reduced (Swarubini et al., 2025).
Building on these advances, many vision-based fall-detection 
methods have been proposed. These methods use different learning 
strategies to analyze video data and detect fall events. Existing ap-
proaches can be broadly divided into several categories. Some methods 
use end-to-end spatio-temporal models, such as 3D convolutional net-
works or transformer-based architectures, to directly learn motion pat-
terns from video sequences. Other approaches rely on object detection 
frameworks, often based on YOLO models, to localize the human body, 
after which postural changes are inferred from bounding-box geometry, 
motion patterns, or additional pose estimation modules to indicate a fall. 
Another group of methods focuses on pose or skeleton-based analysis, 
where body joint trajectories are modeled using recurrent networks, 
graph convolutional networks, or attention mechanisms.
Despite promising results on benchmark datasets, many of these 
methods still present important limitations. Several approaches rely on 
complex multi-stage pipelines or computationally intensive architec-
tures, resulting in increased computational cost and reduced computa-
tional efficiency. Other methods depend on centralized training with 
sensitive video data, increasing reliance on centralized storage and 
motivating the need for decentralized learning frameworks. In addition, 
many existing models lack interpretability and fairness analysis, making 
their decisions difficult to understand and potentially biased in health-
care applications. These limitations motivate the development of light-
weight, explainable, fair, and decentralized fall-detection frameworks 
that support collaborative training while reducing the need for 
centralized storage of raw video data.
Compared with existing fall-detection approaches, this paper pro-
poses an integrated algorithm for reliable and trustworthy elderly fall 
detection that directly addresses several limitations of prior work. 
Rather than relying on multi-stage and computationally demanding 
pipelines involving separate human detection, pose estimation, or 
complex transformer-based models, the proposed framework adopts a 
lightweight end-to-end design based on MobileNetV2 (Sandler et al., 
2018) and a GRU network. This design enables accurate and computa-
tionally efficient fall detection while maintaining a lightweight archi-
tecture. Unlike approaches that rely on fixed-length temporal 
segmentation or short-term motion cues, the proposed GRU-based 
temporal modeling captures motion dynamics across consecutive 
frames, improving robustness to slow and gradual falls. Furthermore, 
while many existing methods depend on centralized training and access 
to raw video data, the proposed framework incorporates federated 
learning to support collaborative model training across distributed data 
sources through model aggregation rather than raw data exchange. This 
decentralized training strategy reduces reliance on centralized storage 
and supports deployment across diverse healthcare and assisted-living 
environments. To further promote trust and transparency, the pro-
posed method incorporates Grad-CAM++ (Chattopadhay et al., 2018) to 
provide interpretable visual explanations of model decisions, allowing 
caregivers and practitioners to understand which discriminative image 
areas influence fall detection outcomes.
Moreover, a fairness evaluation is conducted to assess performance 
consistency across different fall scenarios and user groups, addressing 
ethical considerations that are often overlooked in existing fall-detection 
systems. Overall, this work presents a lightweight, explainable, and 
trustworthy fall-detection framework for elderly monitoring. By 
combining efficient spatial and temporal modeling with decentralized 
training, explainability, and fairness assessment, the proposed frame-
work addresses key challenges related to computational complexity, 
centralized data management, interpretability, and equitable perfor-
mance. Consequently, it provides a promising approach for healthcare 
and assisted-living applications.
Accordingly, the main contributions of this paper are summarized as 
follows: 
1. A lightweight fall detection framework is proposed that combines 
MobileNetV2 for efficient spatial feature extraction with a GRU 
network for capturing temporal motion dynamics, enabling accurate 
and computationally efficient fall recognition.
2. A decentralized federated learning strategy is introduced to enable 
collaborative model training across multiple data sources without 
transferring raw video data, reducing storage burden and supporting 
model generalization across diverse environments.
3. An explainability component using Grad-CAM++ is incorporated, 
which provides visual insights into the spatial regions influencing the 
model’s decisions, improving interpretability, trustworthiness, and 
transparency.
4. A fairness evaluation using Fairlearn is conducted to assess potential 
performance disparities across different subgroups, supporting 
responsible, unbiased, and equitable AI deployment.
5. A practical sliding-window inference mechanism is developed to 
improve computational efficiency while preserving high detection 
accuracy.
6. Comprehensive experiments are evaluated on multi-view datasets to 
validate the effectiveness, robustness, and ethical alignment of the 
proposed fall detection framework.

2. Related work
Existing fall-detection research can be broadly categorized based on 
the sensing modality and modeling strategy used. Early studies explored 
sensor-based approaches, while more recent work has focused on vision- 
based methods, driven by advances in deep learning and the widespread 
availability of cameras.
2.1. Sensor-based fall detection
Some studies relied on non-visual sensors to detect falls. For 
example, Patel et al. (2026) proposed a human fall detection system that 
leveraged ultrasonic sensors and a hybrid deep learning model. The 
authors integrated the Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN), Long 
Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Bidirectional LSTM (BLSTM) to clas-
sify time-series sensor data. This system was embedded within a 
Space-Air-Ground Integrated Network (SAGIN) framework, enabling 
real-time alerts. Similarly, Khan et al. (2025) proposed an explainable 
machine-learning-based fall detection and alert system that integrated 
smartphone sensor data with a cloud-based framework. This approach 
used inertial data captured from smartphone accelerometers and gyro-
scopes processed through a Gradient Boosted Decision Tree (GBDT) 
model to classify falls. Also, the method provided explainability via 
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to clarify how individual fea-
tures influence predictions. While such sensor-based approaches could 
achieve good accuracy under controlled conditions, their real-world 
deployment remains challenging due to device availability, user 
compliance, battery requirements, and sensitivity to noise, limitations 
that are particularly pronounced among older adults.
2.2. Vision-based methods
To overcome the limitations of wearable sensors, more recent studies 
have focused on vision-based fall detection, enabled by advances in deep 
learning and the widespread availability of cameras. These methods 
analyze video data and do not require users to wear any devices.
2.2.1. Appearance features and spatio-temporal modeling methods
Many vision-based approaches rely on appearance features and 
motion patterns extracted from RGB video. Several methods used object 
detection frameworks, particularly YOLO-based models, to detect and 
track individuals and infer falls from changes in body orientation and 
bounding-box motion. Zheng et al. (2024) proposed an improved 
YOLOv8 framework in which the C2f module in the backbone network is 
replaced with the FasterNet module, while a deformable convolution 
module was introduced in the neck section, and a triplet attention 
mechanism was incorporated during multi-scale feature fusion. On the 
other hand, Khekan et al. (2025) proposed a human fall detection 
framework using an improved YOLOv8 model. The model was trained 
and evaluated on the CAUCAFall dataset, which comprised video se-
quences representing various fall types and daily activities in uncon-
strained indoor environments. Furthermore, Chen et al. (2022) proposed 
an elderly fall-detection model based on YOLOv5s. The method replaced 
standard 3 × 3 convolutions with Asymmetric Convolution Blocks (ACB) 
to better extract horizontal, vertical, and rotational features. It inte-
grated a spatial attention module (SAM) to focus on important positional 
cues, and removed the small-scale 76 × 76 detection layer to prioritize 
richer semantic features. In addition, Zaghden et al. (2025) introduced a 
deep learning–based fall detection framework that enhances YOLOv8 
with attention mechanisms to improve feature representation and 
detection accuracy. Furthermore, Sanjalawe et al. (2025) proposed a 
video-based system combining YOLOv8 for spatial human detection and 
Time-Space Transformers (TST) for temporal motion analysis. Using the 
CUCAFall and DiverseFALL10500 datasets, the model detects falls by 
integrating YOLOv8’s real-time object detection with TST’s ability to 
capture motion across frames. Although these methods are effective, 
they are often sensitive to occlusions, camera viewpoint changes, and 
complex backgrounds.
Other studies directly learn spatio-temporal patterns from video se-
quences. In Nú˜nez-Marcos and Arganda-Carreras (2024), Marcos et al. 
proposed a vision-based fall detection framework that leveraged a 
transformer, specifically a Uniformer backbone combining self-attention 
with efficient computation to directly learn fall patterns from raw RGB 
video frames. The model processed short video clips in a sliding-window 
to classify whether a fall had occurred. The authors used two publicly 
available benchmark datasetss; UP-Fall dataset and the UR Fall dataset. 
Similarly, Alam et al. (2025) proposed a vision-based human fall 
detection system using transfer learning with a 3D CNN to capture 
spatio-temporal information from video sequences. A pretrained 3D 
CNN model (trained on Sports-1M) is used as a fixed feature extractor, 
where input videos are segmented into 16-frame clips through the 
network to extract features; these features are then classified using a 
support vector machine (SVM). In a related study, Zou et al. (2021)
introduced a spatio-temporal fall-detection approach that analyzed 
spatial localization and temporal motion patterns. A 3D CNN is inte-
grated with an SSD-based object detection framework to generate con-
strained movement tubes, which track a person through a sequence of 
refined bounding boxes and help mitigate background interference. The 
3D CNN encodes both appearance and motion cues, while additional 
layers perform tube anchor generation, classification, and regression. In 
Fu (2025), Fangping Fu proposed a vision-based fall detection system 
that exploited 3D sparse convolutional neural networks (3D-SCNN). A 
dynamic key-frame selection preprocessing stage is incorporated using 
jitter buffers to adaptively select salient frames. To preserve feature 
continuity before fed to the sparse 3D-CNN model, overlapping 
spatio-temporal cubes are constructed. An alternative approach pre-
sented by Gaya-Morey et al. (2023) proposed an explainable deep 
learning framework for human activity recognition tailored to elderly 
people. This approach leveraged backbone models such as TimeSformer, 
TANet, and TPN for video-based HAR and integrated explainable AI 
(XAI) techniques to generate space and time-aligned explanations that 
make decisions more understandable to caregivers and end users.
Despite their strong performance on benchmark datasets, these 
methods often require high computational resources. Their accuracy can 
also decrease in real-world conditions due to occlusions, lighting 
changes, cluttered backgrounds, and camera viewpoint variations. 
These factors make real-time deployment on low-power devices 
challenging.
2.2.2. Pose and skeleton-based approaches
Another major research direction focuses on human pose or skeleton 
analysis, where fall detection is performed by modeling body joint tra-
jectories. Romaissa et al. (2020) addressed elderly fall detection by 
leveraging human body geometry extracted from video sequences, 
where angular and distance features are calculated between the head 
centroid and the body’s hip center. These features are used to train both 
a two-class SVM and an LSTM network for distinguishing falls from 
normal activities. Related work by Bach et al. (2026) proposed a 
vision-based fall detection method based on YOLOv8n-pose for human 
pose estimation, it incorporated Dynamic Cross-Stage Partial Fusion 
(DyC2F) blocks to enhance feature representation. The temporal dy-
namics of human motion are captured using an LSTM module that 
processes keypoint trajectories. Whereas, Kong et al. (2025) presented a 
vision-based fall detection system that combined deep learning-based 
pose estimation with geometric feature analysis. The method applied 
the YOLOv11 object- and pose-detection model to extract 17 key body 
landmarks from video frames, it computed nine geometric pose angle 
features (e.g., center of mass and joint angles), and trained an SVM 
classifier to detect transitions from upright to lying posture. On the other 
hand, Tran et al. (2025) proposed a fall-detection system that combined 
MediaPipe key-point extraction and either an LSTM network or a 
K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) classifier. The method identifies human falls

joint or skeleton representations, without processing full human images, 
thereby enhancing privacy by minimizing the exposure of identifiable 
visual details. Abdo et al. (2023) proposed a three-stage deep learning 
framework for vision-based fall detection. This method combined YOLO 
for human detection, OpenPose for skeleton extraction, and a Spa-
tial–Temporal Graph Convolutional Network (ST-GCN) for classifying 
fall vs. non-fall events. Whereas, Castro et al. (2023) proposed two 
vision-based fall-detection systems that use 2D and 3D human pose 
estimation combined with an LSTM network enhanced by an attention 
mechanism. Using OpenPose (2D) and FrankMocap (3D), the authors 
extract body-joint coordinates from video frames. While pose-based 
methods offer interpretability and structured motion representation, 
their performance heavily depends on accurate key-point detection and 
degrades under occlusions, poor lighting, or partial body visibility.
Pose-based methods offer better privacy and clearer interpretation of 
body motion, as they do not rely on full human images. However, their 
performance strongly depends on accurate pose estimation. Occlusions, 
poor lighting, and partial body visibility can lead to missing or incorrect 
joint detection, which reduces detection accuracy and robustness.
3. Proposed method
In this work, we introduce a lightweight fall detection framework 
that combines MobileNetV2 for spatial feature extraction, a GRU 
network for temporal motion modeling, and Grad-CAM++ for model 
explainability. The framework further incorporates a federated learning 
strategy to enable decentralized training across distributed data sources 
without sharing raw video data. MobileNetV2 generates compact spatial 
representations with high computational efficiency, while the GRU 
captures motion dynamics across consecutive frames to accurately 
distinguish falls from daily activities. The federated learning framework 
supports collaborative model training across multiple camera sources 
while reducing reliance on centralized storage of personal video data 
and improving generalization across diverse environments. Grad- 
CAM++ is incorporated to provide clear and intuitive visual explana-
tions of the model’s decisions, supporting transparency, trustworthiness, 
and responsible AI principles. Fairness evaluation is also integrated to 
ensure that the system performs consistently across different user groups 
and fall types. The overall system workflow proceeds as follows: 
1. Video streams are captured from surveillance cameras.
2. MobileNetV2 extracts spatial features from individual frames.
3. The GRU processes the sequence of extracted features to learn tem-
poral motion patterns.
4. During training, federated learning aggregates locally trained models 
from multiple clients while keeping the raw video data on the local 
devices.
5. Fairness and explainability components assess the model’s reliability 
and responsible behavior.
6. During testing, a sliding-window mechanism classifies each video 
segment as fall or non-fall, and a fall alert is triggered when two 
consecutive windows are detected as falls.
The following subsections describe each module in detail, along with 
the federated training process and the proposed inference strategy.
3.1. Spatial feature extraction with MobileNet
MobileNetV2 serves as the spatial feature extraction backbone in the 
proposed fall detection framework, providing a lightweight and 
computationally efficient architecture for extracting spatial features 
from video frames. Its core building blocks are inverted residual blocks 
with linear bottlenecks, where each block first expands the input 
channels using a 1 × 1 convolution, applies a 3 × 3 depthwise convo-
lution to capture spatial patterns separately on each channel, and then 
projects the features back to a compact representation using another 1 ×
1 convolution (pointwise) (Sandler et al., 2018). The use of depthwise 
separable convolutions—splitting the spatial filtering (depthwise) and 
channel combination (pointwise)—dramatically reduces the number of 
parameters and floating-point operations compared to standard convo-
lutions, enabling faster inference with minimal loss of accuracy. Mobi-
leNetV2 contains 17 inverted residual blocks, plus initial and final 
convolutions, totaling 53 layers, including all expansion, depthwise, and 
projection layers. Each block preserves essential feature information 
while keeping computations low, generating compact and informative 
spatial feature maps that encode body posture and scene context. These 
features are then passed to the temporal GRU module, which models 
motion dynamics across consecutive frames to accurately distinguish 
falls from normal daily activities.
3.2. Temporal modeling with GRU
Since, MobileNet only processes each frame independently and does 
not connect information across frames, it can miss important temporal 
patterns in videos. Actions like falling happen over several frames, and 
analyzing a single frame alone is often insufficient to accurately detect 
the action. To address this limitation, the proposed technique adds a 
GRU after MobileNet to capture the temporal dependencies between 
consecutive frames.
In this approach, each video frame It, where t denotes the current 
frame index in the video sequence, is first passed through MobileNet to 
extract a feature vector xt=fMobileNet(It), which encodes spatial informa-
tion such as body posture and orientation. The sequence of features x1, 
x2, ...,xT, where T represents the total number of frames in the video, are 
then fed into a GRU, which is a type of RNN designed to capture long- 
term dependencies efficiently. The GRU architecture consists of a hid-
den state ht and two gating mechanisms: the update gate zt and the reset 
gate rt. These gates control how information is passed along the 
sequence, enabling the GRU to remember relevant temporal information 
while ignoring irrelevant details. At each time step, the GRU updates its 
hidden state using the following equations (Dey & Salemt, 2017):

During training, the GRU updates its parameters through Back-
propagation Through Time (BPTT), using the loss between predicted 
and true labels to learn temporal patterns that distinguish abrupt fall 
movements from gradual or routine daily activities.
To support efficient inference, the model operates on the video 
stream using a sequential, non-overlapping sliding-window approach. 
Each window consists of 20 consecutive frames (F1, F2, …,F20), from 
which five frames are uniformly sampled (e.g., F1, F5, F10, F15, F20). 
These sampled frames provide a compact temporal summary while 
reducing computational overhead and redundancy. During inference, 
each sampled sequence is passed through the GRU, where the hidden 
state ht evolves based on learned motion patterns. Sudden posture 
change, such as rapid descent or collapse, lead to substantial updates in 
ht, while smoother motions such as bending or walking produce gradual 
state transitions. The reset gate rt helps discard stale information when 
motion abruptly changes, while the update gate zt determines how much 
new information should override existing memory, enabling the 
network to track the motion trajectory effectively.

classifier to produce a prediction Pw ∈ {fall, non-fall}. To minimize false

3.3. Federated learning for decentralized training
In video-based elderly monitoring applications, multiple cameras 
may be deployed to enable continuous observation and timely fall 
detection. Traditionally, video streams are transmitted to a centralized 
server for data processing and model training. However, transferring 
and storing continuous high-resolution video from multiple devices can 
create significant challenges related to data management, bandwidth 
consumption, and storage requirements. Furthermore, in healthcare and 
assisted-living facilities where numerous individuals are monitored 
simultaneously, these challenges become increasingly pronounced. 
Centralized processing also requires large volumes of video data to be 
collected and maintained at a single location, increasing reliance on 
centralized storage and potentially limiting scalability across diverse 
deployment environments. In addition, handling large video datasets 
may increase network traffic, storage costs, and compliance re-
quirements associated with managing personal healthcare data. These 
challenges motivate the development of decentralized learning frame-
works that support collaborative model training without requiring 
centralized collection of raw video recordings.
To address these limitations, the proposed system adopts a simulated 
federated learning framework (Mammen, 2021), enabling collaborative 
training of fall detection models across multiple video sources while 
reducing reliance on centralized storage of sensitive video data. The 
architecture comprises one central server and five client nodes, each 
representing a distinct camera view or dataset partition, as shown in 
Fig. 1. Each client trains a local model on its data and transmits only 
updated parameters to the central server for aggregation. This decen-
tralized training strategy eliminates the need to transfer raw video data 
to a central repository and enables learning from diverse distributed 
data sources. The federated learning workflow consists of the following 
main steps: 
Step 1: Global Model Initialization:
The central server initializes a global fall detection model and shares 
the initial parameters with all client nodes. 
Step 2: Model Distribution:
The initialized model parameters are distributed to five clients — 
each representing a unique data source. 
• Four clients correspond to distinct camera views from the Le2i 
dataset.
• One client represents the CAUCA dataset, treated as a single entity 
due to its consistent environment and camera configuration.
Step 3: Local Training:
Each client trains the model locally using its own dataset segment. 
Raw video data remain on the client device, reducing reliance on 
centralized storage and lowering data transmission requirements. 
Step 4: Model Updates:
Clients send only the updated model parameters (weights) to the

learning component should be viewed as a decentralized training 
strategy rather than a formally privacy-preserving mechanism.
3.4. Explainability
Misclassifications in fall detection systems for elderly monitoring can 
lead to serious real-world consequences. False positives, when a system 
incorrectly identifies a fall, may cause unnecessary alarms, caregiver 
fatigue, and inefficient use of emergency resources, whereas false neg-
atives, when a true fall is missed, can delay medical response and in-
crease the risk of injury or fatal outcomes. These limitations arise 
because deep learning models often behave as black boxes, providing 
accurate outputs without revealing the reasoning behind their 
predictions.
To overcome this challenge, the proposed research integrates XAI 
techniques within the proposed fall detection framework based on 
MobileNetV2 and a GRU network. Although this hybrid architecture 
achieves strong detection accuracy, its underlying reasoning remains 
opaque. Therefore, Grad-CAM++ is applied to enhance interpretability 
by visually highlighting the image regions that most influence the 
model’s fall or non-fall predictions.
In the proposed model, Grad-CAM++ generates Class Activation 
Maps (CAMs) that assign importance scores to different spatial regions 
in the final convolutional layers of MobileNetV2. Grad-CAM ++ uses the 
last convolutional layer because it captures high-level semantic features 
while preserving spatial information, unlike earlier layers that focus 
only on low-level details (Chattopadhay et al., 2018).
The generated heatmaps are then overlaid on the original video 
frames to indicate which body parts or movements contributed most to 
the classification decision. This visualization provides valuable trans-
parency, allowing developers and caregivers to verify that the system 
bases its predictions on relevant visual cues, thereby improving trust, 
accountability, and safety in elderly fall detection applications. The

4. Experiments and results
This section details the experimental setup and the datasets 
employed in the study, followed by an evaluation of the proposed 
model’s performance across its components. The findings are further 
compared with state-of-the-art models to highlight the strengths of our 
approach.
4.1. Datasets
4.1.1. CAUCAFall
The CAUCAFall dataset, developed by Guerrero et al. (2022), pro-
vides a diverse and realistic collection of fall and non-fall activities 
recorded in an uncontrolled home environment. It features ten partici-
pants, five men and five women, who vary in age, height, weight, and 
clothing style. The recordings were captured using a standard RGB 
camera at 23 frames per second and a resolution of 1080 × 960 pixels, 
resulting in approximately 8 GB of video data. Each subject performed 
five types of falls—forward, backward, left-side, right-side, and from a 
sitting position—and five daily living activities, including walking, 
hopping, object retrieval, sitting, and kneeling. The videos reflect nat-
ural variability in lighting (daylight, artificial, and night conditions), 
occlusions, background motion, and differences in camera angles, dis-
tances, and room textures, making the dataset highly representative of 
real-world home scenarios. Fig. 3(a) shows sample frames from the 
CAUCAFall dataset, illustrating different body postures and fall stages 
captured in a realistic home environment.
4.1.2. Le2i
The Le2i fall detection dataset (L. D. V. Tuyen, 2022) provides 221 
video sequences, containing 131 simulated falls and 90 Activities of 
Daily Living (ADL). Each sequence is recorded with a fixed camera at a 
frame rate of 25 fps and a resolution of 320 × 240 pixels. Different actors 
perform the activities in four environments: home, office, coffee room, 
and lecture room. Out of the total videos, 191 are accompanied by 
manual annotations that supply ground-truth details on fall locations 
and body positions across the image frames. Fig. 3(b) presents sample 
frames from the Le2i dataset, recorded in controlled indoor settings 
across different rooms.
4.2. Experimental setup
The proposed model is trained and evaluated using videos from two 
benchmark fall detection datasets, CAUCAFall and Le2i, with an 80:20 
train–test split. Rather than using each dataset independently, both 
datasets are integrated into a simulated federated learning framework to 
create a heterogeneous client distribution. The Le2i dataset, which in-
cludes recordings from four different indoor rooms, is assigned to four 
clients, while one client represents the CAUCAFall dataset, which con-
tains videos captured under different recording conditions. This client 
partition introduces diversity in scene characteristics, including back-
ground, lighting, camera viewpoints, and recording environments, 
allowing the model to learn from heterogeneous data sources rather than 
from a single dataset. It should be noted that this client configuration 
represents a proof-of-concept simulation and does not reflect a real- 
world federated deployment with independently collected non-IID 
data from geographically distributed institutions or homes.
Within this federated setup, the proposed architecture combines 
MobileNetV2 as a lightweight feature extraction backbone with a GRU 
layer to capture temporal dependencies between frames. The Mobile-
NetV2 component is initialized with pre-trained weights to improve 
convergence and feature quality, while the GRU layer consists of one 
layer with a hidden size of 256. Model training is optimized using the 
Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴ and Cross-Entropy Loss 
as the objective function. During federated training, each client conducts 
one local epoch in every communication round. The global model is then 
updated through 20 rounds of parameter aggregation, with a batch size 
of 4 employed for local training. All experiments were conducted on a 
laptop equipped with an 11th Generation Intel Core i7 processor 
running at 2.3 GHz, 32 GB of RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 
Laptop GPU with 6 GB of dedicated VRAM. The computational perfor-
mance and inference latency reported in this study are therefore specific 
to this hardware configuration. Performance on resource-constrained 
edge devices, such as Raspberry Pi systems, embedded processors, and 
mobile AI accelerators, was not evaluated in the current study.

4.3.2. Computational and deployment analysis
To assess the inference efficiency of the proposed framework, a 
detailed time-cost analysis is conducted on a representative test video 
containing 4204 frames with a duration of 140.13 s. Table 1 presents the 
latency breakdown of the main computational components. Mobile-
NetV2 feature extraction requires 36.22 ms and accounts for 96.8% of 
the total inference time, representing the primary computational cost of 
the framework. In contrast, the GRU temporal modeling module re-
quires only 0.93 ms (2.5% of total inference time), while the fully con-
nected classification layer contributes 0.20 ms (0.5%). It can be noticed 
that the GRU introduces negligible computational overhead because it 
operates on compact feature representations generated by MobileNetV2 
rather than directly on raw image frames. Furthermore, the relatively 
short temporal sequence used in this work substantially reduces the 
computational burden of temporal modeling
Beyond inference efficiency, the communication overhead associ-
ated with federated training is also an important deployment consider-
ation. The proposed federated learning framework requires the 
exchange of model parameters rather than raw video data. The final 
MobileNetV2–GRU model has a disk footprint of approximately 13.5 
MB. During each communication round, every client downloads the 
global model and uploads the locally updated model, resulting in 
approximately 27 MB of communication per client per round. Across the 
20 federated training rounds used in this study, the total communication 
cost is approximately 540 MB per client. Although federated learning 
introduces communication overhead through model exchange, this cost 
is incurred only during training and remains substantially lower than 
continuously transmitting and centrally storing raw video recordings. It 
should be noted that the current study assumes full client participation 
throughout all federated training rounds. Consequently, the impact of 
client dropout and communication failures on model convergence and 
performance is beyond the scope of the present work and was not 
evaluated.
4.3.3. False alarm analysis
False fall alarms may occur in activities that visually resemble falls, 
such as picking up objects from the floor or kneeling as illustrated in 
Fig. 6. In these cases, the system may temporarily classify the movement 
as a fall due to the rapid downward motion of the upper body or a 
sudden change in posture. However, when the individual returns to an 
upright position, the prediction stabilizes, and the false alarm ceases. To 
further minimize these errors, the system confirms a fall event only 
when two consecutive windows are both classified as falls. This rule 
significantly enhances detection reliability by filtering out short or 
ambiguous actions that do not correspond to real fall events. Never-
theless, some daily activities, such as kneeling or bending for a pro-
longed period, may extend across more than two windows, occasionally 
leading to false alarms, as their motion patterns closely resemble 
genuine falls. These cases highlight the difficulty of differentiating be-
tween intentional and accidental downward movements in video-based 
systems. Despite these limitations, the proposed temporal decision 
strategy effectively reduces the frequency and persistence of false 
alarms, maintaining a strong balance between sensitivity to actual falls 
and robustness against normal daily activities.
To quantitatively assess the impact of false detections during 
continuous monitoring, an additional false alarm analysis is conducted 
using long-duration video sequences from the realistic fall dataset of 
Baldewijns et al. (2016). During the 7 h and 34 min of continuous 
monitoring, the proposed framework generates three false alarms, cor-
responding to approximately 0.40 false alarms per hour. Most false de-
tections occur in challenging scenarios, including kneeling, bending, 
and walking activities that occasionally resemble fall-like body postures 
or motion patterns as shown in Fig. 7. Although false alarms may oc-
casionally require additional verification by caregivers, they are 
generally preferable to missed fall events, which may leave an injured 
individual unattended for an extended period. These results provide an 
initial assessment of the framework's behavior under continuous moni-
toring conditions and demonstrate its ability to maintain a relatively low 
false-alarm rate while preserving high sensitivity to fall events. How-
ever, the current evaluation is conducted on benchmark data rather than 
real-world residential deployments. Therefore, further validation 
through long-term monitoring in healthcare and assisted-living envi-
ronments remains necessary to fully assess false-alarm rates and prac-
tical deployment performance.

4.3.5. Fairness evaluation
When a model is unfair or biased, it may misclassify certain groups 
more frequently, leading to unequal safety outcomes, such as delayed 
detection for one gender or fall type. So, this raises the importance of 
responsible AI when designing AI applications to guarantee the model 
output is explainable and fair. So, in this paper, the model performance 
predictions are evaluated to ensure that the system performs equitably 
across all users, regardless of factors such as gender, age, or fall direc-
tion. Therefore, by assessing fairness alongside accuracy, developers can 
identify hidden biases, improve model reliability, and promote trust and 
accountability in the proposed model. To support this objective, Fair-
learn (an open source library) (Fairlearn) is used in the proposed system 
to assess the fairness of the model. The fairness is evaluated through two 
aspects: gender (male and female) and fall direction (back, front, side, 
and from a chair). Fig. 9 presents the Fairlearn evaluation of model 
performance across the fall directions. It can be noticed that Fairlearn 
results show consistent F1, recall, and precision across all fall directions, 
indicating that the model performs fairly and accurately without bias 
toward any specific fall type (Beya et al., 2015).
Moreover, the model predictions are evaluated across the gender of 
the fallen persons, and the results are shown in Fig. 10. The results show 
nearly identical F1, recall, and precision scores for male and female 
subjects, indicating that the fall detection model performs equitably 
across genders without observable bias.
Furthermore, Fig. 11 presents the combined fairness evaluation of 
the proposed fall detection model across both gender and fall types. This 
figure examines the interaction between the two factors to determine 
whether performance disparities appear in specific combinations, such 
as female-side or male-chair falls. The results show that the model

4.5. Ablation study
Ablation experiments are conducted to examine the impact of tem-
poral configuration on both the training and inference stages of the 
proposed fall detection model. Two complementary studies are per-
formed: (1) an ablation study on training temporal context and (2) an 
ablation study on window configuration and latency. Together, these 
experiments aim to identify the temporal setup that best balances ac-
curacy, responsiveness, and robustness.
In the first study, the effect of temporal input length on model per-
formance is evaluated using three configurations: Model 20, Model 40, 
and the Full Model. Models 20 and 40 are trained using sliding windows 
of 20 and 40 consecutive frames, respectively, while the Full Model is 
trained on entire video sequences without segmentation. For fair com-
parison, all models are tested using 20-frame sliding windows. As shown 
in Table 3, Model 40 achieves a very high recall for the Fall class (0.99), 
indicating that nearly all true fall events are detected; however, its 
precision drops substantially (0.65), suggesting a high number of false 
positives. In contrast, Model 20 demonstrates a more balanced perfor-
mance, with a recall of 0.89 and a precision of 0.72 for the Fall class, 
capturing shorter fall events effectively but missing longer temporal 
patterns. The Full Model outperforms both window-based models across 
all metrics, maintaining high precision and recall for both Fall and Not 
Fall classes (precision: 0.92–0.99, recall: 0.97–0.98), even when evalu-
ated on 20-frame windows. This indicates that exposure to complete 
motion sequences during training enables the model to learn richer 
temporal patterns and contextual cues, allowing it to accurately recog-
nize partial motion sequences at inference.
These results in Table 3 highlight the importance of incorporating 
global temporal information during training. While longer sliding win-
dows improve sensitivity, training on full video sequences provides the 
best trade-off between precision and recall, yielding the most robust and 
generalizable configuration across the evaluated benchmark datasets.
To optimize both accuracy and computational efficiency, additional 
experiments examine the influence of temporal inference settings on the 
proposed fall detection system. Specifically, these studies explore how 
window size, step size (number of frames processed per inference), and 
the number of consecutive windows required to confirm a fall affect 
performance. These parameters are carefully tuned to balance F1-score 
with inference latency, defined as the number of frames needed to 
produce a detection, which is critical for timely intervention. Window 
size determines the temporal context the model observes, with larger 
windows capturing longer motion sequences but introducing delays, 
while step size controls prediction frequency, with smaller steps 
enhancing responsiveness. Successive window confirmation is employed 
to filter out transient misclassifications caused by brief non-fall move-
ments, such as bending or sitting.
The results in Table 4 reveal that a 20-frame window, processed 5 
frames per step, and double confirmation for fall events achieve the 
optimal balance between sensitivity and speed. In this configuration, a 
fall is confirmed only after two consecutive positive windows, whereas 
non-fall events require a single negative window, allowing the system to 
respond faster to normal activity while maintaining reliability for fall 
detection. This setup achieves a high F1-score of 0.98, with inference 
latencies of 20 frames for non-fall and 40 frames for fall events. Smaller 
windows reduce latency without sacrificing accuracy, whereas larger 
windows introduce unnecessary delay, highlighting the importance of 
carefully balancing temporal context and responsiveness.
Together, these ablation studies underscore the value of global 
temporal information during training and efficient short-window 
inference during deployment. By integrating full-sequence training 
with a 20-frame sliding-window inference strategy and a double- 
confirmation mechanism, the system achieves a well-balanced synergy 
of precision, robustness, and rapid decision-making. This configuration 
enables the model to capture rich temporal patterns during training 
while maintaining low-latency, high-confidence predictions during 
deployment. Complementing these design choices, the system achieves 
an inference throughput of approximately 93 FPS, demonstrating high 
computational efficiency while maintaining accurate fall detection.

5. Conclusion
This paper presents a lightweight and explainable fall detection 
framework that integrates MobileNetV2 for spatial feature extraction, 
GRU-based temporal modeling, Grad-CAM++ for visual interpret-
ability, and federated learning for decentralized model training. The 
proposed framework achieves competitive performance on the CAUCA 
Fall and Le2i benchmark datasets while maintaining high inference ef-
ficiency. Experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the 
MobileNetV2–GRU architecture for distinguishing fall events from daily 
activities, while Grad-CAM++ provides intuitive visual explanations 
that enhance transparency and trust in the model’s predictions. 
Furthermore, fairness evaluation confirms consistent performance 
across different fall scenarios and user groups.
The federated learning strategy enables collaborative model training 
across distributed data sources without requiring centralized collection 
of raw video recordings. Although centralized training achieves slightly 
higher accuracy, the federated model maintains competitive perfor-
mance while reducing reliance on centralized storage and transmission 
of sensitive personal video data. Computational analysis further dem-
onstrates the efficiency of the proposed framework, and continuous- 
monitoring experiments provide an initial assessment of false alarms 
under realistic operating conditions.
Despite these promising results, several limitations remain. The 
current evaluation relies on publicly available benchmark datasets, 
while the federated learning framework is assessed through a proof-of- 
concept simulation. Therefore, additional validation in real-world 
healthcare and assisted-living environments is necessary to establish 
the framework's suitability for practical clinical deployment. In addi-
tion, false alarms may still occur during challenging daily activities 
which can exhibit motion patterns similar to falls. Furthermore, while 
federated learning reduces reliance on centralized storage of raw video 
data, the proposed framework does not incorporate formal privacy- 
preserving mechanisms and therefore does not provide formal privacy 
guarantees. Future work will focus on large-scale validation in real 
healthcare and assisted-living environments, investigating robustness 
under heterogeneous deployment conditions, reducing false alarms 
during daily activities, and integrating advanced privacy-enhancing 
mechanisms within the federated learning framework. These di-
rections will further strengthen the practical applicability, trustworthi-
ness, and responsible deployment of AI-based fall detection systems.
